Adrian Serrin: “I’ve Never Been Very Interested in Being the Smartest Person in the Room”

The Halden Ledger sits down with Avelor founder Adrian Serrin to talk about ambition, failure, spaceflight, money, and what he looks for in the people around him.

By Marcus Venn  |  The Halden Ledger

The following conversation has been edited for length and clarity.

HALDEN LEDGER: Avelor has become one of the most visible aerospace companies in the world. Do you still think of yourself as an engineer?
ADRIAN SERRIN: More than anything else, probably.
HALDEN: Even now?
SERRIN: Especially now. The job changes, but the habit doesn’t. You look at a system, figure out what it’s doing, figure out what it should be doing, and then start closing the gap.
HALDEN: That makes running a company sound very mechanical.
SERRIN: People usually say that when they think engineering is about machines. Most of it is about tradeoffs.
HALDEN: Were you always ambitious?
SERRIN: No. Curious, yes. Restless, definitely. Ambition came later.
HALDEN: What changed?
SERRIN: I realized that being right about something and being able to do something about it were two different skills.
HALDEN: Avelor gave you the second one?
SERRIN: Avelor gave me a place to build things that were too expensive, too complicated, or too early for almost anyone else to want.
HALDEN: That sounds like a terrible business plan.
SERRIN: It was, for a while.
HALDEN: What do you actually enjoy about running Avelor?
SERRIN: The early part of things. When nobody knows exactly what the answer is yet.
HALDEN: And the part you enjoy least?
SERRIN: Meetings where everyone already agrees.
HALDEN: Why?
SERRIN: Either the problem is trivial or somebody isn’t talking.
HALDEN: You have a reputation for hiring people who disagree with you.
SERRIN: I try to.
HALDEN: That sounds exhausting.
SERRIN: It’s cheaper than being wrong.
HALDEN: What makes someone interesting to you in an interview?
SERRIN: Someone who can say, “I don’t know,” without apologizing for it.
HALDEN: That’s unusual in aerospace.
SERRIN: That’s unusual everywhere.
HALDEN: What about intelligence?
SERRIN: Useful. Overrated by intelligent people.
HALDEN: What matters more?
SERRIN: Judgment. Curiosity. Knowing when the evidence has changed enough that you should change your mind.
HALDEN: Do you change your mind easily?
SERRIN: No.
HALDEN: That sounded very self-aware.
SERRIN: I’ve had practice.
HALDEN: What were you like before Avelor?
SERRIN: Broke.
HALDEN: Beyond financially.
SERRIN: Less patient. More convinced I could solve everything myself.
HALDEN: Can you?
SERRIN: No. That was an expensive lesson.
HALDEN: What was the first thing you bought when you had real money?
SERRIN: A decent mattress.
HALDEN: Really?
SERRIN: Absolutely. I had been sleeping on something that was probably violating international law.
HALDEN: No sports car?
SERRIN: Eventually.
HALDEN: There it is.
SERRIN: I didn’t say I became enlightened.
HALDEN: Why spaceflight?
SERRIN: Because access changes everything.
HALDEN: Access to space?
SERRIN: Access to anything. If something is expensive enough or difficult enough to reach, only a few people get to decide what happens there. Lower the cost, improve reliability, increase frequency, and suddenly the number of possible uses explodes.
HALDEN: Is that what Avelor is trying to do?
SERRIN: In the simplest terms, yes.
HALDEN: You make it sound almost mundane.
SERRIN: That’s the goal.
HALDEN: Spaceflight becoming mundane?
SERRIN: Routine is more valuable than spectacular.
HALDEN: That may be the least romantic description of spaceflight I’ve ever heard.
SERRIN: Romantic gets you a documentary. Routine gets you infrastructure.
HALDEN: Do you enjoy being wealthy?
SERRIN: Parts of it.
HALDEN: Which parts?
SERRIN: Not thinking about ordinary emergencies. A broken transmission isn’t a crisis. A medical bill isn’t a crisis. Your parents need something, you do it. People who have always had money underestimate how much mental space that buys you.
HALDEN: And the parts you don’t enjoy?
SERRIN: People deciding who you are before they’ve met you.
HALDEN: Do you think you would like you if you read about yourself?
SERRIN: Probably not.
HALDEN: Why?
SERRIN: The version of me that gets written about is usually much more certain than I am.
HALDEN: You’re perceived as very confident.
SERRIN: Confidence and certainty aren’t the same thing.
HALDEN: What’s the difference?
SERRIN: Confidence is being willing to make a decision when you don’t have everything you want. Certainty is pretending you do.
HALDEN: What scares you?
SERRIN: Waste.
HALDEN: Of money?
SERRIN: Time.
HALDEN: You think about time a lot?
SERRIN: Everybody does. Most people just call it something else.
HALDEN: Do you have hobbies?
SERRIN: Chess, occasionally. Hiking. I cook badly.
HALDEN: Badly by your standards or objectively badly?
SERRIN: Objectively. I have witnesses.
HALDEN: What do you cook?
SERRIN: Things that become other things.
HALDEN: That’s not encouraging.
SERRIN: It’s honest.
HALDEN: What would surprise people about you?
SERRIN: I don’t know. I don’t spend much time imagining what people think about me.
HALDEN: I don’t believe that.
SERRIN: That’s fair.
HALDEN: What do you want Avelor to be remembered for?
SERRIN: I don’t particularly care if Avelor is remembered.
HALDEN: That’s a strange thing for a founder to say.
SERRIN: Companies aren’t people. They’re tools. If Avelor is useful for thirty years and then something better replaces it, that’s a good outcome.
HALDEN: And what do you want to be remembered for?
SERRIN: Ask me when I’m older.
HALDEN: You don’t have an answer?
SERRIN: I have answers. That’s different.
HALDEN: Last question. Are you happy?
SERRIN: That’s an unfair last question.
HALDEN: You can decline.
SERRIN: No. I think I am.
HALDEN: You think?
SERRIN: See? That’s why it was unfair.
