Can Machines Build the Machines?

A new generation of automated industrial systems is being designed to do more than repeat a task. The harder goal is to let one production system help create the next.

By Marcus Venn  |  The Halden Ledger
An automated assembly cell used in advanced manufacturing research. The current systems still rely heavily on human-supplied parts, tooling and supervision. Halden Ledger file image.

On the assembly floor, the system can already do a surprising amount on its own.

It inspects parts, moves components between stations, performs several assembly steps, tests the result and routes failed units back for rework. Some of the equipment it helps assemble can later be used elsewhere in the same production network. But the raw materials still arrive from outside suppliers, the tooling was designed by people, and technicians still step in whenever the process reaches the edge of what the software understands.

No one here is pretending that the machine is building a copy of itself. The more interesting question is narrower and more practical: how much of an industrial system can be built, maintained or expanded by the industrial system itself?

Even with those limits, the project points toward a manufacturing problem that has occupied robotics researchers for years: how much of an industrial system can be built and expanded by the industrial system itself?

The question has become more practical as companies including Nyvolis Technologies and Avelor Astronautics invest in automated construction, remote maintenance and equipment designed for places where adding another shift of workers is difficult or expensive.

On Earth, that can mean a remote mine or an automated warehouse. Beyond Earth, the economics become harder to ignore.

The bottleneck is not the robot

Industrial robots are already common. They weld vehicle frames, move pallets, inspect circuit boards and perform repetitive operations with speed and consistency that human workers would struggle to match.

Those machines are usually surrounded by an enormous amount of human-built support. Someone installs the line, calibrates the tooling, replaces worn components, updates the software, clears jams, manages inventory and decides what the factory should make next.

Removing a worker from one station does not remove that surrounding system.

The newer work is pushing outward into that surrounding system.

Engineers are combining automated inspection, modular tooling, machine vision, mobile robotics and software that can reassign work when equipment fails. The ambition is not a factory that operates without people. It is a factory that needs fewer people standing next to every machine in order to keep functioning.

Off-world industry makes those support systems harder to ignore. A broken actuator on Earth can be replaced from a warehouse. On the Moon, the same failure may wait for the next cargo flight unless somebody planned for it months earlier.

Anika Rao starts with failure

Anika Rao is one of the engineers working on that problem.

Rao's background is in automated manufacturing and systems integration, the part of engineering where a machine that works perfectly by itself still counts as a failure if it cannot survive the factory around it.

Engineer Anika Rao during a robotics systems demonstration. Her team is studying how automated equipment can inspect, maintain and support other machines in the same production environment. Halden Ledger file image.

During a recent demonstration, Rao spent more time talking about what the system could not do than what it could.

One manipulator could exchange its own end tool, but it could not repair the tool. A mobile platform could deliver parts between stations, but only within a mapped work area. The inspection system could identify several classes of defect, though unfamiliar failures still went to a human technician.

“The impressive demo is the machine doing the task,” Rao said. “The useful demo is what happens when the task goes wrong.”

It is a less spectacular benchmark, and a more useful one for judging whether the system can scale.

Building capacity, not copies

“Self-replicating factory” appears often in discussions of advanced automation, though it describes current systems poorly.

A modern factory depends on too many specialized inputs for literal self-replication to be a useful near-term target. Semiconductors, bearings, sensors, lubricants, precision optics and high-performance materials all come from supply chains that stretch far beyond a single building.

Researchers are concentrating on a narrower question: how much useful capacity can an automated system add to the network around it?

A robotic work cell might assemble additional mobile platforms from delivered parts. Those platforms could move material, inspect equipment or prepare new work areas. A fabrication system might produce brackets, housings or structural pieces used to install another production line. Automated excavation equipment could prepare a site where more equipment will later operate.

None of those machines has reproduced itself, but the industrial network has still grown. For engineers, that is the more useful measure.

Why the Moon changes the math

The engineering is difficult; the economic incentive is easier to see.

Every machine delivered to a lunar settlement occupies launch capacity. So does every replacement part, every repair tool and every additional piece of construction equipment. A system capable of producing even a modest fraction of those items locally can reduce the amount of mass that has to be delivered later.

The first targets are likely to be simple.

Structural members do not require semiconductor fabrication. Regolith handling does not require a factory capable of producing precision optics. Basic shielding, site preparation, storage structures and some classes of mechanical replacement parts can be made with much less industrial depth than a complete spacecraft.

Every additional material that can be sourced locally gives the automation more work it can perform without another launch.

That is one reason lunar infrastructure projects such as Themelion matter to robotics researchers. A permanent site creates repeated maintenance and construction work in a place where labor, spare parts and transportation are unusually expensive.

Permanent lunar infrastructure gives the technology a demanding place to prove whether those gains are real.

The parts still have to come from somewhere

Moving parts with robots does not eliminate the supply-chain problem.

A factory that can assemble ten machines from imported components still depends on imported components. If one motor controller comes from Taiwan, a bearing from Germany and a sensor package from California, the production line is only as autonomous as its least replaceable part.

A useful measure of autonomy is therefore not simply how many tasks can run without a worker standing nearby. It is how much of the production system can keep operating when a component fails, a shipment is late or a machine encounters something outside its normal routine.

That is where modularity becomes important. Standardized motors, common fasteners, interchangeable controllers and machines designed around replaceable subassemblies can make a limited inventory support more equipment.

It is less elegant than a machine building an identical copy of itself, but much closer to how real factories expand.

A factory that can help build a factory

A meaningful threshold could arrive well before full industrial independence.

Imagine a remote site with one excavation unit, one materials processor and several general-purpose mobile robots. If that equipment can prepare foundations, move feedstock, fabricate simple structural components and install part of a second production area, the site has gained something important: new capacity no longer requires every kilogram of supporting infrastructure to arrive finished.

Humans would still design the expansion. Imported electronics and precision components would still be required, and technicians would still solve failures the machines could not. What changes is the ratio between imported effort and locally created capacity.

One shipment supports more construction, one technician supervises more machines, and one production line helps install another.

For a warehouse in Nevada, that may improve margins. For a lunar base, it can determine whether growth is practical at all.

The prototype question

Emerging technologies are often judged too generously after a successful demonstration.

A prototype proves that a machine can work. A factory proves that somebody believes it matters.

The automated systems now being tested remain heavily dependent on conventional supply chains, human supervision and controlled environments. They are not autonomous industrial ecosystems, and the engineers closest to them generally do not describe them that way.

The work is now moving beyond isolated tasks.

Automation is moving outward from individual tasks toward the infrastructure around those tasks: inspection, maintenance, logistics, fabrication and expansion.

Rao put it more simply.

“If every new machine requires another team of people standing beside it,” she said, “you have automated a machine. You have not automated growth.”

The machines on the floor are not there yet, but they are beginning to show what the next step might require.
