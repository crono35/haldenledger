Bitcoin Was Supposed to Be Outside the System — But Has It Now Become the System?

Institutional funds, corporate treasuries and governments have embraced an asset built to operate beyond their control. What happens when the alternative becomes part of the establishment?

By Marcus Venn  |  The Halden Ledger

Bitcoin began as an argument against the financial system. Eighteen years later, the financial system may be one of its biggest beneficiaries. An asset designed to let people hold and transfer value without relying on banks is now sold through brokerage accounts, packaged into investment funds, held on corporate balance sheets and discussed by governments as a possible strategic asset.

The network itself has not changed much in that respect. Bitcoin still operates without a central bank. Ownership can still be transferred without asking a financial institution for permission, and its supply rules are still written into software rather than set by a monetary policy committee. What changed was everything around it.

For a growing number of investors, Bitcoin does not mean setting up a wallet or securing a private key. It means clicking “buy” in the same account that holds stocks, bonds and retirement savings. Technically, Bitcoin still sits outside much of the traditional financial system. In practice, it is increasingly hard to tell where one ends and the other begins.

The rebellion got a ticker symbol

Early Bitcoin advocates placed enormous importance on the absence of an intermediary. A person could hold an asset without placing it in a bank and transfer it without depending on one. That remains possible, but the financial industry eventually learned that many people were less interested in using Bitcoin than in owning something whose price moved with it.

That was an opportunity. Asset managers built funds. Brokers offered access. Custodians took responsibility for storage. Wealth managers began recommending small allocations to clients who had no interest in learning how a hardware wallet worked.

Bitcoin’s financialization did not require anyone to change Bitcoin. It only required investors to prefer convenience. Today, someone can own exposure to an asset famous for self-custody without ever taking custody of anything, and for a large part of the market, that is exactly the point.

Owning Bitcoin is not always the same as holding Bitcoin

Direct ownership carries responsibilities that traditional investment products are designed to remove. Lose the credentials to a wallet and there may be nobody to call. Send funds to the wrong address and there is no dispute department. A poorly planned inheritance can leave an asset effectively inaccessible even when everyone involved knows it exists.

Those risks have always been part of the tradeoff. Institutions offer a familiar alternative. They handle custody, reporting, security and access, while the investor gets a financial product whose value tracks the underlying asset. That arrangement looks much more like conventional finance, but it is also easier.

Bitcoin made intermediaries unnecessary. It did not make them unattractive.

A hedge against what?

Bitcoin has been called a lot of things: electronic cash, digital gold, speculative technology, an inflation hedge, a store of value and a censorship-resistant network. Depending on when someone bought it, several of those descriptions may have appeared correct.

The problem is that Bitcoin has never behaved consistently enough to fit neatly into any one category. Investors who see it as digital gold usually begin with scarcity. The protocol limits the amount that can ultimately exist. Governments cannot increase that supply because the economy is weak, a banking system is under stress or a war is expensive.

That makes for a simple argument, and the counterargument is simple too: scarcity is only valuable if someone wants what is scarce. Bitcoin has no mechanism for solving that problem. The market does.

If people continue to see value in holding an independently scarce digital asset, the limit matters. If they do not, the mathematics remain exactly the same. That is why arguments over Bitcoin so often end up being arguments over trust. The technology can define supply; it cannot define value.

Governments changed the conversation

For years, government discussions of cryptocurrency tended to revolve around fraud, sanctions, tax evasion, consumer protection and criminal finance. Those concerns never disappeared, but the conversation became more complicated as ownership spread into institutions that governments already regulate.

Once pension funds, public companies, banks and millions of ordinary investors have exposure, the question is no longer simply whether cryptocurrency should be kept at a distance. Regulators now have to decide how banks can custody it, how funds holding it should operate, how gains should be taxed and what governments themselves should do with bitcoin they acquire or seize.

That is a very different debate from the one taking place a decade ago. Bitcoin may exist outside central banking, but it no longer exists outside financial policy. Its price affects investors. Its custodians operate under regulation. Its exchanges rely on banking relationships. Its products sit inside retirement and brokerage accounts.

That does not give governments control of Bitcoin. It gives them considerable influence over the roads most people use to reach it.

Wall Street did not capture Bitcoin

There is a neat version of this story in which Bitcoin started as a rebellion and Wall Street eventually took it over. Reality is less satisfying.

Banks cannot create more bitcoin. Fund managers cannot rewrite transactions. Governments cannot call the network administrator and order a transfer reversed. The underlying system continues to do what it was designed to do.

Wall Street entered because there was money to be made serving people who wanted access to it, and investors accepted Wall Street’s involvement because traditional institutions solved problems many of them did not want to solve themselves. That is not exactly a defeat for Bitcoin, but it is not exactly the original vision either.

A decentralized network can support a heavily institutionalized market. Bitcoin is now showing what that looks like.

The energy argument is still here

Bitcoin mining uses a significant amount of electricity, and after years of debate there is still little agreement over what that fact should mean. Supporters point to miners using inexpensive, stranded or underused power, and to operations that can reduce consumption quickly when local demand rises.

Critics make a more basic argument: electricity used for mining is still electricity being consumed. The disagreement becomes difficult to settle because the two sides are often answering different questions. One side asks whether mining can use energy efficiently. The other asks whether mining is a worthwhile use of energy in the first place.

No engineering study can answer the second question. Societies use enormous amounts of power on things they consider worthwhile and object to much smaller uses they do not. Bitcoin’s energy debate has always contained a debate about Bitcoin itself.

Success created a different kind of dependence

Bitcoin does not need permission from a bank to keep operating, but its market increasingly does. Large volumes of trading now pass through exchanges, custodians, brokerages and investment products that depend on banking access, regulatory approval and legal infrastructure.

Governments can change tax treatment. Regulators can restrict products. Banks can sever relationships with exchanges. Custodians can be subjected to new requirements. None of those things switch Bitcoin off, and they do not have to.

They can still change how easily ordinary investors can buy it, hold it or sell it. The network may be decentralized, but access to the network is becoming much less so.

Becoming normal may have been the bigger change

The most remarkable thing about Bitcoin in 2027 may be how little remark it now attracts. Its price runs beside equity indexes and commodities. Retirement accounts can hold exposure. Corporate finance departments discuss allocations. Banks offer custody. Analysts publish price targets.

None of that would have sounded ordinary when Bitcoin first appeared. Now much of it does, which leaves an awkward question. If banks, funds, corporations and governments eventually decide Bitcoin belongs inside the financial system, does that mean Bitcoin succeeded? Or does it mean the financial system succeeded at absorbing something built to escape it?

There may not be much difference anymore. Bitcoin did not replace traditional finance. Traditional finance learned how to live with Bitcoin, and increasingly, it may have learned that it would rather have Bitcoin inside the system than outside it.
